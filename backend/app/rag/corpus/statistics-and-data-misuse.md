# Statistics and data misuse

## Missing denominators

"1,000 people who took X later got sick" means nothing without the base
rate. How many took X in total? How many who did not take X got sick? A
raw count with no denominator is a rhetorical device, not evidence.

## Relative versus absolute risk

"Doubles your risk" can mean a rise from 1-in-a-million to 2-in-a-million.
Credible coverage gives both the relative change and the absolute numbers.

## Truncated and inverted axes

Charts that start the y-axis above zero exaggerate small differences.
Inverted axes reverse the apparent trend. Always read the axis labels
before the shape.

## Unrepresentative samples

Online polls, self-selected respondents, and tiny sample sizes cannot
support population-level claims. Check n, how subjects were recruited, and
the margin of error.

## P-hacking and single studies

One study is a data point, not a conclusion. Look for replication, sample
size, pre-registration, and whether the finding survived peer review and
meta-analysis. Beware "a new study proves" framing.
