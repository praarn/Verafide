# Sensationalism and emotional manipulation

## What it looks like

Fabricated and misleading content leans heavily on emotional arousal
because outrage, fear, and vindication travel faster than measured
reporting. Tells include ALL-CAPS words mid-sentence, stacked exclamation
marks, loaded adjectives ("shocking", "disgusting", "evil"), and urgency
cues ("share before this is deleted", "they don't want you to see this").
Legitimate reporting can cover emotional events, but it usually attributes
the emotion to named people rather than instructing the reader how to feel.

## Why it works

Emotional content suppresses analytical scrutiny. A reader who is angry is
less likely to check a byline, look for a dateline, or ask who is quoted.
Manufactured urgency ("act now") removes the pause in which verification
would normally happen.

## How to check

Separate the claim from its framing. Restate the core factual assertion in
neutral language and ask whether it is still newsworthy or verifiable
without the adjectives. Look for a named author, a publication date, and
at least one on-the-record source. If removing the emotional language
leaves nothing checkable, treat the piece as rhetoric, not reporting.
